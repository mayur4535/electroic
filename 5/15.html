Semi-Conductor JEDEC Color Code Diagrams & Theory

During the early days of solid-state electronics, printing sharp, readable text on tiny curved transistor packages (such as classic TO-92 plastic housings or metallic epoxy capsules) was difficult. To overcome this limitation, manufacturers adapted the standard resistor color-coding standard to label transistors.

Under the standardized Joint Electron Device Engineering Council (JEDEC) system, the prefix "2N" represents a three-lead active semiconductor device (such as bipolar junction transistors or field-effect transistors). Therefore, the color bands represent only the remaining numbers of the registration standard. When you view a transistor with three or four color bands, you can read them sequentially from top to bottom (away from the lead wires) to find the numeric sequence.

Formulas & Mathematical Logic

JEDEC Rule: Device Code = "2N" + (Band 1 Digit) + (Band 2 Digit) + (Band 3 Digit) + [Band 4 Digit, if present]
Classic Diodes (Alternative): Standard JEDEC diodes use "1N" prefix, using the same color band digit alignment.
Color Digit mapping follows the global international standard: Black=0, Brown=1, Red=2, Orange=3, Yellow=4, Green=5, Blue=6, Violet=7, Grey=8, White=9.

Step-by-Step Example

Example: 4-Band Transistor - Orange, White, Black, Yellow
Step 1: Write down the implied base prefix for a transistor: "2N".
Step 2: Note the band digit equivalents: Orange = 3, White = 9, Black = 0, Yellow = 4.
Step 3: Concatenate the sequence: 3, 9, 0, and 4 to form 3904.
Step 4: Combine the components to identify the registered industry part: 2N3904.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What does the "2N" prefix on a transistor stand for?

Under JEDEC notation, "2N" denotes a component with two internal active semiconductor junctions, which corresponds to standard three-terminal transistors. Diodes use "1N" (one junction).

2. Why did old transistors use color bands instead of printed text?

Early automated printing machinery struggled to place durable, legible text on small, curved plastic or metal surfaces. Color bands were cheap, highly visible, and did not smudge during assembly.

3. Can I use this calculator for diodes?

Yes, JEDEC diodes follow the same sequence. However, you must swap the "2N" prefix with "1N" (e.g., Brown, Black, Black, Black would represent a 1N1000 diode).

4. Why doesn't my transistor have a multiplier band?

Unlike resistors or inductors, JEDEC transistors are registered by sequential part numbers. Thus, the color bands represent standard, direct integers rather than multipliers or tolerances.

5. What standard is used for JEDEC transistor color codes?

They follow EIA standard color digit mappings, where colors correspond to digits 0 through 9 sequentially from Black to White.
