Formulas & Mathematical Logic

AWG sizes calculate based on a logarithmic sequence between standard baseline anchor points.

AWG diameter formula: d_n = 0.127 × 92^((36 - n)/39) mm
AWG area formula: Area = π × (d_n)^2 / 4

SWG sizing was historically based on raw physical drawing dies, whereas AWG follows an exact geometric ratio where each successive slot represents a logical increment in cross-sectional surface area.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why do SWG and AWG sizing parameters differ?

AWG is based on an exact geometric progression of wire drawing dies, where each size reduces by a fixed ratio. SWG was developed historically using raw non-geometric increments that were later standardized by the British government.

2. Which wire gauge is larger: 14 AWG or 14 SWG?

14 SWG is larger. It measures exactly 2.03 mm in diameter, whereas 14 AWG measures 1.63 mm in diameter.

3. What do the 7/0 and 6/0 representations mean in SWG?

These are standard cable size codes. 7/0 represents 0000000 gauge, which is a very thick heavy duty power conductor measuring exactly 12.7 mm (0.5 inches) in diameter.

4. Why does a smaller wire gauge number mean a thicker wire?

Gauge sizes originally represented the number of times a raw rod had to be pulled through drawing dies to achieve its size. More pulls resulted in a thinner wire, which is why larger numbers represent thinner wires.

5. Can I substitute SWG and AWG wire sizes directly?

Direct substitution can lead to problems because their physical diameters differ. Always use the closest larger gauge size in the system you are converting to to avoid voltage drop or heating issues.

6. Why do the smaller wire sizes of SWG show '---' values in the chart?

SWG sizing traditionally stopped at smaller wire boundaries. For extremely thin sizes (like 51 to 56), only the American Wire Gauge (AWG) provides standard industry dimensions.
