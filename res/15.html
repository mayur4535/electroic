Input Parameters Specification

dBm (Decibel-Milliwatts)The logarithmic ratio of power representing active signal intensity relative to one milliwatt (1 mW).
System Impedance (Ω)The characteristic resistance of the transmission line, commonly 50 ohms for RF antennas or 75 ohms for coaxial video runs.
RMS Voltage (V)Root-Mean-Square voltage equivalent, representing the effective alternating voltage level of the signal.
Peak-to-Peak Voltage (V_pp)The complete vertical voltage swing between the maximum positive and maximum negative peaks of the wave.

Practical Operational Examples

Mobile Cell Receiver Sensitivity

A typical mobile phone receiver receives signal levels around -90 dBm, which translates to a micro-faint 10 picowatts of power.

Wi-Fi Antenna Transmitter

Commercial Wi-Fi routers transmit signals at roughly +20 dBm, representing 100 milliwatts (0.1 W) of active radiated power.

Coaxial TV Cable Sizing

Video coax runs use 75-ohm systems. A signal level of 0 dBm equates to 1 mW of power and yields an RMS voltage of 273.9 millivolts.

Laboratory Signal Checks

Standard laboratory signal generators output +10 dBm into 50-ohm loads, yielding an RMS voltage of exactly 0.707 Volts.

Formulas & Mathematical Logic

Converting logarithmic decibels back to linear electrical units relies on fundamental power equations.

Power calculation: Power (Watts) = 10^((dBm - 30) / 10)
RMS Voltage calculation: V_rms = sqrt(Power × Impedance)
Peak Voltage calculation: V_peak = V_rms × sqrt(2)
Peak-to-Peak Voltage: V_pp = V_peak × 2

Because the decibel scale is logarithmic, every +10 dB increase represents a tenfold (10x) increase in power, while every +3 dB increase doubles the physical power output.

About This Calculator

Instantly convert logarithmic dBm values to linear Watts and Volts.

The CalcBoy dBm to Voltage and Power Converter translates RF signal parameters across standard impedance systems.

In RF (Radio Frequency), wireless engineering, and high-frequency electronics, signal power is almost always measured on a logarithmic scale called dBm (decibels relative to one milliwatt). This logarithmic system makes calculating path losses and system gains much easier, as complex multiplications are turned into simple additions and subtractions.

However, when designing hardware interfaces, checking outputs with oscilloscopes, or evaluating circuit component tolerances, engineers need to know the equivalent linear voltage limits (RMS, Peak, and Peak-to-Peak) and power in Watts. Because these voltage values depend on the system's characteristic impedance, having a dynamic conversion utility is critical.

This calculator performs these conversions instantly. Users can enter any standard dBm value, select 50-ohm or 75-ohm layouts, and view precise equivalent current values, helping with lab testing, signal line design, and system debugging.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is dBm?

dBm is a unit of power level expressed in decibels (dB) relative to one milliwatt (1 mW). It is used to measure absolute signal power in telecommunications and radio systems.

2. Why does impedance affect the voltage in a dBm system?

dBm measures absolute power, not voltage. Since power depends on both voltage and current (P = V^2 / R), changing the system impedance (R) directly alters the resulting voltage level.

3. What is the standard impedance for RF and Video cables?

Broadcasting antennas, mobile equipment, and laboratory RF instruments strictly use 50-ohm systems, while television coaxial runs and consumer video lines use 75-ohm cables.

4. What is the conversion from dBm to dBW?

dBW is relative to 1 Watt instead of 1 milliwatt. Since 1 Watt is 1,000 times more powerful than 1 mW, the conversion is simple: dBW = dBm - 30.

5. How do you find Peak-to-Peak voltage from RMS voltage?

Assuming a standard sinusoidal wave shape, the Peak-to-Peak voltage is calculated by multiplying the RMS voltage by the square root of 2 (to get Peak voltage) and then multiplying by 2 (V_pp = V_rms × 2.8284).

6. Can this tool calculate non-sinusoidal waveforms?

No. This converter assumes standard sinusoidal alternating current waveforms. Square waves, triangular signals, or complex pulse sequences have different Peak-to-RMS ratios (crest factors).
