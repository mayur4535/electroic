Formulas & Mathematical Logic

Power Ratio (dB) = 10 * log10(Power Output / Power Input)
If power is in mW: W = mW * 0.001
Linear Ratio = Power Output / Power Input

Step-by-Step Example

Example: Power Output = 10 W, Power Input = 50 mW.
Step 1: Convert all power inputs to a standard matching unit of Watts: Power Output (Pout) = 10 W, Power Input (Pin) = 50 mW * 0.001 = 0.05 W.
Step 2: Calculate the linear power ratio: Linear Ratio = Pout / Pin = 10 / 0.05 = 200.
Step 3: Apply the logarithmic base-10 conversion equation to convert the linear ratio to decibels (dB): Power Ratio (dB) = 10 * log10(Linear Ratio) = 10 * log10(200) = 10 * 2.30103 = 23.01 dB.
Result: The computed parameters are Linear Ratio = 200.00x and Power Ratio = 23.01 dB, indicating the exact power gain step through the amplifier.

About This Calculator

Quantify radio frequency system gain and attenuation using standard logarithmic ratios.

The CalcBoy RF Power Ratio Conversion Calculator converts linear power ratios into decibels (dB), allowing designers to analyze transmitter gains, channel losses, and signal level variations.

In radio frequency (RF) design, system performance is characterized by how much a signal is amplified or attenuated as it travels through active components like power amplifiers, or passive components like attenuators and cables. Comparing the raw input power (Pin) and output power (Pout) as a simple linear fraction (Pout / Pin) provides the linear ratio, indicating how many times the signal has increased or decreased. However, because signal power across a complete transmitter or receiver chain can vary by several orders of magnitude, engineers use logarithmic decibel (dB) scales to represent these ratios. Decibels convert complex multiplications and divisions of cascaded components into simple additions and subtractions, greatly simplifying link budget planning.

This calculator acts as a highly precise RF parameter solver, helping engineers translate between linear power ratios and logarithmic decibels. By allowing the input of both input and output powers in milliwatts (mW) or Watts (W), the tool handles all required unit conversions internally before applying the standard power ratio equation. Whether you are measuring the gain of a low-noise amplifier (LNA) or the insertion loss of a microstrip bandpass filter, this tool ensures accurate, error-free ratio evaluations, securing system design integrity across high-frequency transmission networks.

Frequently Asked Questions

What physically is an RF power ratio, and why is it measured in decibels (dB)?

An RF power ratio is the comparison of output power to input power. Decibels (dB) are used because electromagnetic signals lose strength exponentially over distance. Logarithmic decibel scales compress these huge ranges into linear, easily manageable numbers, allowing cascaded system gains and losses to be simply added or subtracted.

What is the difference between dB, dBm, and dBW?

The unit dB is a relative ratio with no units (e.g., gain or loss). The units dBm and dBW are absolute power levels referenced to a specific physical value. The unit dBm is referenced to 1 milliwatt, while dBW is referenced to 1 Watt. You can add dB (ratio) to dBm (absolute power) to find the output power in dBm, but you cannot add dBm to dBm directly.

Why does the power ratio formula use 10 * log10 instead of 20 * log10?

The multiplier 10 is used because we are calculating a power ratio. Decibels for power ratios are defined as 10 * log10(Pout / Pin). For voltage or current ratios, decibels are defined as 20 * log10(Vout / Vin) because power is proportional to the square of voltage.

What happens if the calculated power ratio is negative?

A negative decibel value indicates that the output power is less than the input power, meaning the system has suffered signal attenuation or insertion loss. For example, a power ratio of -3 dB means the output power is half of the input power (0.5x).

Can this calculator be used for signal combining systems?

Yes. If you are combining multiple signal paths, you can input the total combined output power and the input branch power to calculate the active combining gain or loss across the system networks.

How does impedance affect the calculated power ratio?

Because the power ratio is a direct comparison of input and output power, it is independent of system impedance, provided both the input and output ports are matched to the same characteristic impedance (typically 50 Ohms) to prevent reflection losses.
