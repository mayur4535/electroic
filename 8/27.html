Diagrams & Theory

Formulas & Mathematical Logic

Free Space Loss = C + 20 * log10(Distance) + 20 * log10(Frequency)
ERP = Tx Power - Tx Cable Loss + Tx Antenna Gain
Rx Signal Strength = ERP - Free Space Loss + Rx Antenna Gain - Rx Cable Loss
Fade Margin = Rx Signal Strength - Rx Sensitivity

Constants adjust dynamically matching environment scale vectors (C = 36.6 for standard miles tracks / C = 32.5 for kilometer metrics).

Step-by-Step Example

Example: Frequency = 2440 MHz, Tx Power = 23 dBm, Tx Cable Loss = 0.9 dB, Tx Antenna Gain = 9 dB, Distance = 10 miles, Rx Antenna Gain = 24 dB, Rx Cable Loss = 0.9 dB, Rx Sensitivity = -90 dBm, Compute target = Fade Margin.
Step 1: Calculate the Free Space Loss (FSL) using the constant factor for miles (C = 36.6): FSL = 36.6 + 20 * log10(Distance) + 20 * log10(Frequency) = 36.6 + 20 * log10(10) + 20 * log10(2440) = 36.6 + 20 + 67.7 = 124.3 dB.
Step 2: Calculate the Effective Radiated Power (ERP) at the transmitter antenna: ERP = Tx Power - Tx Cable Loss + Tx Antenna Gain = 23 - 0.9 + 9 = 31.1 dBm.
Step 3: Calculate the Received Signal Strength (RSSI) at the receiver: RSSI = ERP - FSL + Rx Antenna Gain - Rx Cable Loss = 31.1 - 124.3 + 24 - 0.9 = -70.1 dBm.
Step 4: Calculate the total link Fade Margin: Fade Margin = RSSI - Rx Sensitivity = -70.1 - (-90) = 19.9 dB.
Result: The computed link budget yields a Free Space Loss of 124.3 dB, an RSSI of -70.1 dBm, and a safe Fade Margin of 19.9 dB (yielding a Good link status).

About This Calculator

Optimize wireless system designs, evaluate transmission path dynamics, and establish reliable link safety margins.

The CalcBoy RF Link Budget Calculator evaluates the signal path performance of wireless communication links. It computes either the link fade margin, the maximum transmission distance, or the required transmitter output power using multi-parameter inputs.

An RF link budget is a comprehensive mathematical accounting of all the power gains and losses that an electromagnetic signal experiences as it propagates from a transmitter through an open-air medium to a receiver. Designing a robust wireless network—whether it is a terrestrial Wi-Fi link, a long-range point-to-point microwave backhaul, or a satellite communication transponder—requires a detailed link budget analysis. If the signal reaching the receiving antenna is too weak, it falls below the receiver sensitivity threshold, resulting in packet loss, link degradation, or a complete connection dropout. An accurate budget ensures that there is sufficient margin to withstand temporary environmental attenuations.

To compute these transmission metrics, the calculator integrates several distinct physical layers. The active power of the transmitter radio is attenuated by the passive insertion losses of coaxial cables, connectors, and lightning protectors before reaching the feedpoint. The antenna then concentrates this energy, providing a directional gain (Gt). As the wave travels through free space, it spreads spherically, creating a high-loss propagation path (Free Space Loss) before being captured by the receiver's directional antenna (Gr) and attenuated again by receiver cabling. The net signal level reaching the receiver port—referred to as the Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI)—must exceed the receiver sensitivity by a safety threshold called the fade margin. This calculator solves for these variables, allowing RF engineers to predict link reliability under diverse operational configurations.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the physical meaning of an RF link budget, and why is it important?

An RF link budget is a detailed accounting of all the power gains and losses in a communication path from the transmitter to the receiver. It is critical because it determines whether the received signal is strong enough to be decoded by the receiver while keeping a safety margin against environmental attenuations.

What is a Fade Margin, and what is an acceptable minimum level?

Fade margin is the safety margin (in dB) between the received signal level (RSSI) and the minimum receiver sensitivity. It protects the link against signal drops caused by weather (rain, fog), multi-path reflections, or physical obstructions. A fade margin of 15 to 20 dB is generally targeted for high-reliability systems.

How does the choice of distance units (miles vs. km) affect the calculation?

The unit choice changes the free-space propagation constant used in the loss formula. For calculations in miles, the constant is 36.6, whereas for kilometers, the constant is 32.5. Selecting different units internally scales the mathematical equation to ensure the output remains physically accurate.

Why does cable loss significantly degrade the link budget?

Coaxial cables have internal resistive and dielectric losses. As high-frequency currents travel down the line, some RF power is dissipated as heat. This attenuation directly reduces the net power reaching the transmitter antenna (lowering EIRP) and attenuates the signal captured by the receiver antenna before it reaches the receiver port.

Can this calculator reverse-engineer the required transmitter power or distance?

Yes. The calculator features an OPTIONS selector at the top. By switching the Compute target to "Distance" or "Tx Power," the tool mathematically rearranges the logarithmic link equations to solve for the maximum allowable path distance or the minimum transmitter power required to maintain your target fade margin.

What is the physical meaning of the Link Status ratings (Strong, Good, Weak, Poor)?

These ratings categorize the link reliability based on the calculated fade margin. A margin above 20 dB is "Strong" (immune to most weather changes), 15 to 20 dB is "Good" (highly reliable), 10 to 15 dB is "Weak" (vulnerable to fading), and below 10 dB is "Poor" (prone to frequent dropouts).
