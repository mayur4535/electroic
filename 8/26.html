Step-by-Step Example

Example: Transmitter Power = 100 mW, Transmitter Gain = 2 dBi, Receiver Gain = 2 dBi, Frequency = 2.4 GHz, Distance = 10 m.
Step 1: Convert units to standard physical forms: Transmitter Power (Pt) = 100 mW = 0.1 W, Transmitter Gain (Gt) = 10^(2/10) = 1.5849, Receiver Gain (Gr) = 10^(2/10) = 1.5849.
Step 2: Calculate the wave propagation wavelength: lambda = c / f = 300,000,000 / 2,400,000,000 = 0.125 m.
Step 3: Apply Friis transmission equation parameters: Pr = Pt * Gt * Gr * (lambda / (4 * pi * d))^2 = 0.1 * 1.5849 * 1.5849 * (0.125 / (4 * pi * 10))^2.
Step 4: Solve the mathematical equation to find received power: Pr = 0.25119 * (0.125 / 125.6637)^2 = 0.25119 * 9.8946e-7 = 2.4854e-7 W.
Result: Convert Watts back to mW to match input units: Received Power = 2.4854e-7 * 1000 = 2.4854e-4 mW.

About This Calculator

Calculate received power and analyze line-of-sight RF link budgets with scientific precision.

The CalcBoy Friis Transmission Calculator evaluates the received power (Pr) of a wireless link using transmitter output power, transmitter/receiver antenna gains, operating frequency, and distance variables.

The Friis Transmission Equation is one of the most fundamental formulas in radio frequency (RF) engineering, telecommunications, and satellite link design. Developed by Danish-American radio engineer Harald T. Friis in 1945, the equation calculates the power received by an antenna under idealized free-space conditions from another transmitting antenna located at a specific distance. Rather than looking only at transmitter output power, the Friis formulation integrates transmitter antenna gain, receiver antenna gain, wavelength, and distance to model the complete path of electromagnetic energy propagation.

To perform accurate link calculations, the formula converts logarithmic decibel values (dBi) into linear gain terms and rescales input powers. The wavelength of the signal is derived from the operating frequency and the speed of light. As the signal propagates through free space, the wavefront spreads out spherically, causing the power density to decrease with the square of the distance. The Friis equation solves this relationship, helping RF engineers, network planners, and satellite communication technicians design link budgets, establish signal-to-noise ratios, and specify minimum receiver sensitivity requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the physical meaning of the Friis Transmission Equation?

The Friis Transmission Equation calculates the power received by an antenna from a transmitting antenna at a given distance under free-space conditions. It assumes an unobstructed line-of-sight path and perfectly matched, lossless antenna structures.

Why does received power decrease at higher frequencies in the Friis equation?

The physical path loss of free space does not increase with frequency. However, because higher-frequency signals have shorter wavelengths, a standard antenna with dimensions proportional to the wavelength becomes physically smaller. This smaller aperture captures less of the expanding spherical wavefront, which reduces the total received power.

How do transmitter and receiver antenna gains affect the received power?

Antenna gains (Gt and Gr) represent the directional focusing ability of the antennas. Rather than radiating or receiving energy uniformly in all directions, high-gain antennas squeeze the energy into a narrow beam, significantly increasing the power density in the main direction of propagation and raising the received power.

What is the relationship between the Friis equation and Free-Space Path Loss (FSPL)?

The two are closely related. Free-Space Path Loss is the geometric attenuation of a signal propagating through space. The Friis equation is essentially a complete link budget that calculates the net received power by taking the transmitter power, adding transmitter antenna gain, subtracting Free-Space Path Loss, and adding receiver antenna gain.

Can this calculator be used for indoor wireless networks?

Only as a baseline. Indoor environments do not represent ideal free-space conditions due to multiple reflections (multipath fading), absorption from walls, and scattering from furniture. Indoor path loss is typically much higher, and received power will be lower than the Friis equation predicts.

What happens if the calculated received power is below the receiver sensitivity?

If the received power falls below the receiver sensitivity limit, the system will be unable to decode the signal, resulting in data corruption, dropped packets, or complete link failure. Designers must increase transmitter power, use higher-gain antennas, or reduce the transmission distance to maintain the link.
