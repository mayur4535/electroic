Formulas & Mathematical Logic

TpW = Lb / Dw. Turns per winding layer depends on bobbin length and wire diameter.
W = N / TpW. Number of winding layers is total turns divided by turns per layer.
Dc = 2 × W × Dw + Db. Final coil diameter includes both sides of winding thickness plus bobbin diameter.
Dcin = Dc / 25.4. Coil diameter is converted from millimeters to inches.
Rw = (W × Dw + Db) / 2. Average winding radius is used for estimating wire length.
A = π × Rw × Rw. Cross-sectional area is estimated from the average radius.
L = 2 × π × Rw × N / 1000. Total wire length is based on average turn circumference and total turns.
R = RpM × L, V = I × R, P = V × I. Resistance, voltage and power are calculated from wire resistance and DC current.

Step-by-Step Example

Example values: wire diameter = 0.5 mm, turns = 500, bobbin length = 25 mm, bobbin diameter = 10 mm, current = 0.3 A.
Turns per layer: TpW = 25 / 0.5 = 50 turns.
Winding layers: W = 500 / 50 = 10 layers.
Final coil diameter: Dc = 2 × 10 × 0.5 + 10 = 20 mm.
Average radius: Rw = (10 × 0.5 + 10) / 2 = 7.5 mm.
Wire length: L = 2 × π × 7.5 × 500 / 1000 = about 23.56 m.
Practical meaning: the coil will need roughly 23.56 meters of wire and the final heating depends on current and calculated resistance.

This CalcBoy coil calculator estimates the physical and electrical properties of a wound coil using wire diameter, turns, bobbin size and DC current.

The Coil Physical Properties Calculator is made for practical coil winding work where both the mechanical shape and electrical behavior matter. A coil is not only a number of turns: it has winding layers, final outside diameter, average turn length, copper wire length, resistance, voltage drop and heating power. This tool combines these important values into one fast calculation for solenoids, electromagnets, relay coils, actuator coils and general copper winding projects.

Key OutputsTurns per layer, winding layers, coil diameter, wire length, resistance, voltage and power.
Design BenefitCompare wire diameter and turn count before physically winding the coil.
Safety ReminderAlways check temperature rise because coil power becomes heat in DC operation.

In a real coil, wire diameter affects almost everything. Thinner wire allows more turns in a small space, but resistance increases and heating can become a problem. Thicker wire lowers resistance and can carry more current, but it increases winding size and may reduce how many turns fit across the bobbin length. Bobbin diameter and length also change the total wire length because each layer sits farther from the center as the coil grows.

Quick tip: after calculating resistance and power, always test the finished coil with a multimeter and run a short temperature check before continuous operation.

This calculator is a strong starting point for coil design, but final values can vary because enamel thickness, winding tightness, insulation layers, wire material, bobbin shape, air gaps and temperature all affect the finished result. For high-current coils, electromagnets or solenoids that stay energized for a long time, use a safe current limit, adequate wire gauge, ventilation and proper thermal testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does this coil calculator calculate?

It estimates winding layers, final coil diameter, cross-sectional area, total wire length, resistance, voltage and power from basic coil inputs.

Why does wire diameter matter?

Wire diameter affects how many turns fit in one layer, how many layers are needed, total resistance and coil heating.

Can I use this for solenoid coils?

Yes. It is useful for solenoid coil planning, but final force also depends on core material, air gap, magnetic path and current.

Does the calculator include enamel thickness?

No. It uses entered wire diameter directly. If enamel insulation is significant, enter the outside diameter including insulation for a more practical estimate.

Why is coil power important?

In DC coils, electrical power mostly becomes heat. High power can overheat the coil, melt insulation or damage nearby parts.

Can I use this for electromagnets?

Yes. It helps estimate winding size and electrical demand, but magnetic strength also depends on core geometry, material and saturation.
