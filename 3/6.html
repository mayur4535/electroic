Diagrams & Theory

Electrical length is the physical length of a transmission line expressed in terms of the wavelength of the signal traveling through it. One complete wave cycle has a phase transition of 360 electrical degrees, which corresponds exactly to one wavelength (λ).

Formulas & Mathematical Logic

Conductor Signal Speed: v = VF × c (where c ≈ 299,792,458 m/s)
Physical Wavelength: λ = v / Frequency (Hz)
Electrical-to-Physical Conversion: Length (meters) = (Electrical Degrees / 360) × λ
Step-by-Step Conversion Example (1370 kHz at 90° with VF = 0.95):
• Step 1: Convert frequency to Hz: 1370 kHz = 1,370,000 Hz
• Step 2: Compute wave speed in conductor: 299,792,458 × 0.95 = 284,802,835 m/s
• Step 3: Compute wavelength: 284,802,835 / 1,370,000 = 207.885 meters
• Step 4: Calculate physical length: (90 / 360) × 207.885 = 51.971 meters
• Step 5: Convert to feet: 51.971 × 3.28084 = 170.51 feet

About This Calculator

Translate phase parameters into physical dimensions for radio antennas and waveguide feeders.

The CalcBoy Electrical Degrees to Meters Converter calculates electrical length conversions, generating standard physical conductor lengths, wavelengths, and imperial measurements.

In RF (Radio Frequency) and microwave design, physical dimensions are heavily dictated by the frequency of operation. Rather than measuring antennas, delay lines, and waveguides exclusively in meters, engineers often specify lengths in "electrical degrees". This approach directly relates physical length to the phase shift experienced by a signal as it propagates through a medium.

The translation depends on the velocity of the signal. In a vacuum, radio waves travel at the speed of light ($c \approx 299,792,458$ m/s). However, inside physical conductors, coaxial cables, or microstrip trace paths, signals travel slower. This velocity reduction is captured by the Velocity Factor (VF). For standard broadcast antennas and steel tower configurations, a default VF of 0.95 is conventionally applied to adjust calculations.

This calculator performs precise wave evaluations, giving engineers, antenna designers, and broadcast technicians a reliable tool to plan RF structures, analyze feeder cables, and comply with FCC antenna height audits.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is an electrical degree in RF systems?

An electrical degree is a unit used to express physical length in terms of wave phase shift. A full wave cycle corresponds to 360 electrical degrees, which is equal to one physical wavelength (λ) inside the propagating medium.

2. How does the Velocity Factor (VF) affect physical length?

Signals propagate slower through physical conductors and cables than in a vacuum. The Velocity Factor reduces the wave speed ($v = VF \times c$), which shortens the physical wavelength and the required conductor length.

3. Why does the FCC list AM tower heights in electrical degrees?

AM radio performance depends on the radiation pattern, which is dictated by the electrical height of the tower relative to the signal wavelength. Measuring height in electrical degrees provides a standard metric independent of the station's frequency.

4. What is a typical Velocity Factor for coaxial cables?

Typical velocity factors range from 0.66 for solid polyethylene (PE) dielectric cables to 0.80–0.88 for foamed polyethylene, and up to 0.95–0.98 for air-spaced coaxial lines.

5. How does a 90° tower compare to a 180° tower?

A 90° tower is a quarter-wavelength antenna ($\lambda / 4$), which is the most common vertical radiator. A 180° tower is a half-wavelength antenna ($\lambda / 2$), which provides a lower radiation angle and higher ground-wave signal strength, but requires twice the physical height.

6. Can we have an electrical length exceeding 360 degrees?

Yes. Electrical lengths exceeding 360 degrees represent conductors that are physically longer than one wavelength. This is common in long transmission lines, delay lines, and multi-wavelength traveling-wave antennas.
