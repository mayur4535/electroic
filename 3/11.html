Diagrams & Theory

Transformers utilize magnetic flux loops within steel laminations to transfer electrical energy across circuits. Winding loops must be wound uniformly around core limbs to limit leakage inductance and maintain inductive coupling.

Formulas & Mathematical Logic

Single-Phase Apparent Power (VA): VA = V * I
Three-Phase Apparent Power (VA): VA = sqrt(3) * V * I
Single-Phase Power Capacity: kVA = (V * I) / 1000
Three-Phase Power Capacity: kVA = (sqrt(3) * V * I) / 1000

Where:

  • V = Primary Line-to-Line Voltage in Volts
  • I = Primary Full-Load Current in Amperes
  • sqrt(3) = Phase factor multiplier, mathematically constant at approximately 1.7320508

Step-by-Step Example

Example Scenario: Selected Configuration = Three Phase (3Φ), Voltage = 480 V, Current = 150 A.
Step 1: Check phase selection. Because Three-Phase is selected, apply the phase multiplier of 1.7320508.
Step 2: Multiply voltage, current, and the phase factor: VA = 1.7320508 * 480 * 150 = 124,707.6 VA.
Step 3: Convert Volt-Amperes to Kilo-Volt-Amperes by dividing by 1000: kVA = 124,707.6 / 1000 = 124.7 kVA.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why does the three-phase kVA formula multiply by 1.732?

The factor 1.732 is the square root of 3 (√3). It accounts for line-to-line relationships in balanced three-phase electrical networks where phase waveforms are separated by 120 electrical degrees.

2. Can I input secondary voltages and currents into this calculator?

Yes. The mathematical relationship between voltage, current, and kVA is identical for both primary and secondary windings of a transformer.

3. Why is transformer capacity rated in kVA instead of kW?

Transformers are rated in apparent power (kVA) because internal conductor heat dissipation depends on raw current and voltage values, regardless of the load's power factor (PF).

4. Does this calculator support unbalanced phase systems?

No. This calculator operates under the standard engineering assumption of perfectly balanced line-to-line currents. Unbalanced systems require evaluating individual phases separately.

5. What happens if the calculation results in a negative value?

If negative terminal parameters are provided, the calculator displays the resulting negative power value as-is. This behavior helps engineers inspect phase directional flow offsets during modeling.
