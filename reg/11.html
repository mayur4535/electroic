Diagrams & Theory

Voltage drop happens when conductor resistance reduces delivered voltage between the power source and load. Longer cable, smaller diameter and higher current increase wire loss.

Formulas & Mathematical Logic

Wire area: A = π * d² / 4
Cable resistance: R = 2 * resistivity * length / area
Voltage drop: Vdrop = current * resistance
Three phase adjustment: Vdrop = Vdrop * 1.732 / 2
Voltage drop percent: Vdrop percent = Vdrop / supply voltage * 100

The calculator converts AWG, inch or millimeter wire diameter into meters, converts feet into meters when required, then computes resistance, voltage drop and percentage loss.

Step-by-Step Example

Example: Finding the voltage drop for a 12V DC copper line carrying 15A of current over a one-way distance of 25 feet using a 10 AWG wire.
Step 1: Identify conductor resistivity (Copper = 1.72 * 10^-8 Ohm-meters).
Step 2: Determine wire cross-sectional area: For 10 AWG, diameter is approx 2.588 mm, yielding an area of pi * (0.002588)^2 / 4 = 5.26 * 10^-6 square meters.
Step 3: Calculate the total round-trip path resistance: R = 2 * (1.72 * 10^-8) * 7.62 meters (25 feet) / (5.26 * 10^-6) = 0.0498 Ohms.
Step 4: Calculate the voltage drop under a 15A load: Vdrop = 15A * 0.0498 Ohms = 0.747V.
Step 5: Express as a percentage of the source: (0.747V / 12V) * 100 = 6.2% drop (exceeding standard 3% recommendations).

About This Calculator

Optimize electrical cable sizing and calculate potential drops across AC and DC paths using CalcBoy's professional suite.

This design tool estimates voltage drop, percentage power loss, and total conductor path resistance to comply with National Electrical Code (NEC) sizing standards.

Selecting the correct wire size goes far beyond simply matching the current-carrying capacity (ampacity) of a conductor. In any electrical system, current flowing through a wire encounters natural physical resistance. Over long distances, this resistance acts as a load in series, dropping voltage before it can reach the target device. This potential drop is particularly problematic in low-voltage DC applications (like solar, automotive, and marine networks) where a drop of even 1 Volt represents a massive percentage of the total operating voltage.

According to Joule's Law, power dissipated as heat in a wire is proportional to the square of the current (I^2 * R). Undersized conductors operating under heavy currents lead to dangerous heating, risking melted insulation and electrical fires. The National Electrical Code (NEC) recommends keeping total voltage drop under 3% for branch circuits and 5% for overall feeder combinations to maintain safe, efficient operating limits across electrical panels and motor hubs.

By entering the primary cell and wire specifications, the tool resolves storage voltage, total path resistance, and percentage losses. This allows engineers and DIY solar installers to select correct wire gauges before assembling custom panels or running high-power feeders.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is voltage drop in electrical cables?

Voltage drop represents the loss of electrical potential along a conductor path caused by the inherent physical resistance of the wire material. This resistance dissipates energy as heat, reducing the voltage delivered to the load.

2. What is the maximum acceptable voltage drop percentage under standard codes?

According to standard National Electrical Code (NEC) guidelines, the maximum recommended voltage drop is 3% for branch circuits and 5% for the combined feeder and branch circuit system to maintain efficiency.

3. Why is voltage drop more severe in low-voltage DC systems?

A 1V drop is minor in a 120V circuit (0.83%), but represents 8.3% of a 12V system. This significant percentage loss drastically reduces device efficiency and can prevent equipment from operating.

4. How does aluminium compare to copper as a conductor?

Aluminium is lighter and cheaper but has about 60% higher resistivity than copper. Consequently, aluminium cables must be larger in cross-sectional area to achieve the same low voltage drop as copper lines.

5. Why does this calculator multiply the cable length by two for DC and single-phase runs?

In DC and single-phase AC circuits, current must travel out to the load and return via the neutral/negative conductor. This represents twice the one-way cable length, which the calculator includes automatically.

6. How does ambient temperature affect cable resistance and voltage drop?

As temperature rises, the atoms in the conductor vibrate more, obstructing the flow of electrons. This increases resistance and elevates the overall voltage drop under heavy, continuous loads.
